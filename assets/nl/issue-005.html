The big story: the women's basketball money, school by school

Last week we did the men. Women's college basketball tipped off too, so we pulled the same two years of federal filings, 2023-24 (FY2024) and 2024-25 (FY2025), for all 68 Power 4 schools plus Notre Dame (68 total). Source: U.S. Dept. of Education EADA, 2024 and 2025 data · Our scoreboard

The headline: women's basketball revenue across the 68 schools rose from $294.7M to $320.6M, up $25.9M (8.8%). Spending rose from $440.3M to $472.8M, up $32.5M (7.4%). The typical (median) program brought in $4.55M and spent $6.65M.

For scale: the same 68 schools reported $1.26B in men's basketball revenue. Women's hoops brought in about a quarter of that, but schools spent about 47% as much on it ($472.8M vs. $1.01B).

The top 5 in women's basketball revenue, FY2025:

  • South Carolina: $11.5M to $13.1M (+$1.6M)

  • Cal: $6.1M to $10.8M (+$4.6M, +76%)

  • UCLA: $7.8M to $9.7M (+$1.9M)

  • USC: $7.3M to $9.6M (+$2.4M)

  • TCU: $5.2M to $9.6M (+$4.4M, +86%)

South Carolina is still the standard. The Gamecocks led the Power 4 in women's hoops revenue and spending ($13.1M each), and they're one of just two schools that spent more on women's basketball than on men's (South Carolina $13.1M vs. $11.1M). The other: LSU, at $12.1M women's vs. $11.1M men's.

Only two programs reported making money: Purdue and Louisville. Purdue reported $8.5M in revenue against $4.9M in spending (+$3.6M). Louisville reported $8.2M against $6.7M (+$1.5M). That's it. In FY2024, it was also just two.

The biggest gaps between spending and revenue:

  • LSU: $4.1M revenue, $12.1M spent (−$8.0M)

  • Texas: $2.5M revenue, $10.4M spent (−$7.9M)

  • Maryland: $1.4M revenue, $8.2M spent (−$6.8M)

  • Arkansas: $0.3M revenue, $6.7M spent (−$6.4M)

  • Oklahoma: $2.6M revenue, $8.8M spent (−$6.1M)

  • Duke: $6.2M revenue, $12.3M spent (−$6.1M)

Biggest revenue jumps: Cal (+$4.6M) and TCU (+$4.4M), then SMU (+$2.7M), USC (+$2.4M), Arizona State and Arizona (+$2.3M each). Biggest drops: Georgia (−$1.8M), Iowa (−$1.7M), Stanford, NC State and Illinois (about −$1.5M to −$1.6M each).

Duke spent the most new money. Duke's women's basketball spending rose $3.1M to $12.3M, the second-most in the Power 4. Only Cal's increase was bigger (+$4.6M).

⚠️ Read this before you dunk on anyone. Revenue minus expenses is NOT profit, and it's not a ranking of who "loses money." 33 of the 68 programs reported women's basketball revenue exactly equal to expenses in FY2025, including South Carolina, Cal, UCLA, USC and Notre Dame. At those schools, "revenue" can include money the school moved in to cover the bill. That's also why some of the biggest revenue "jumps" (Cal, SMU, USC) match their spending increases dollar for dollar. And these are 2024-25 numbers, the last season before schools started paying players directly.

NIL & revenue-sharing news

1. Tennessee basketball players put out the first model college sports CBA. On Nov. 10, Tennessee's men's basketball players and Athletes.org released a non-binding term sheet for what a collective bargaining agreement could look like: a salary cap paired with a spending floor, guaranteed minimum pay for every rostered player, 100% of guaranteed pay for injured players, a 15-day transfer window, and caps on agent fees. It isn't ratified, the players haven't formed a union, and Tennessee's administration says it hasn't agreed to or endorsed the terms. Front Office Sports reports one spending-floor option is a negotiated percentage of the athletic budget. A football version is in the works. (Athletes.org, Nov. 10 · AP · Front Office Sports)

Our numbers on it: Tennessee reported $285.4M in athletic revenue for 2024-25, so every 1% of budget is about $2.85M. Men's basketball brought in $35.3M, the most in the SEC, against $20.2M in spending. (EADA FY2025)

2. The SEC is weighing a luxury tax on schools that pay players over the cap. ESPN reports a working group of SEC athletic directors, presidents and lawyers is considering taxing schools based on how far they go over the $21.3M revenue-sharing cap, and the league would like to vote before football's early signing period, Dec. 2-4. (Athletic Business, citing ESPN, Nov. 8)

Our numbers on it: the $21.3M cap is about 10% of the typical SEC school's 2024-25 revenue ($204.9M median). It ranges from 6% of Texas's budget to 15% of Vanderbilt's. (EADA FY2025)

3. NIL Go cleared $192.6M in deals in September and October. The College Sports Commission says 10,092 deals cleared and 581 worth $20.2M didn't. 73% of the cleared money ($140.5M) came from "associated" entities, like collectives and boosters tied to schools, and the CSC says deals are getting more complicated, including school money routed through third-party "sponsors." Since NIL Go launched in June 2025: 56,570 deals worth $775.1M cleared. (College Sports Commission, Nov. 9 · On3)

Our numbers on it: $775.1M in cleared deals over 17 months is about 6.5% of the $12.0B our 68 schools reported in athletic revenue for 2024-25 alone. (EADA FY2025)

4. Rutgers is keeping Greg Schiano, and the buyout is part of why. Rutgers announced Nov. 10 that Schiano will return in 2027 despite a 2-7 record. His buyout after this season would be about $17.3M to $18.9M, depending on the outlet's math, dropping to about $11.7M after 2027. CBS Sports reports Rutgers athletics ran a $78M deficit in 2024-25. (Rutgers Athletics, Nov. 10 · USA TODAY · CBS Sports)

Our numbers on it: Rutgers reported $75.9M in football spending in 2024-25, matched exactly by reported football revenue. An $18.9M buyout would be about 25% of that, and about 11% of Rutgers' $171.7M department total. (EADA FY2025)

5. Two ADs say the old money model is over. Louisville AD Josh Heird told WDRB "the days of self-sustaining athletic departments are in the rearview mirror"; Louisville projected a fiscal 2027 deficit approaching $30M and may borrow to cover it. In Baton Rouge, LSU AD Verge Ausberry warned of a "cliff," saying football teams could jump from $50M to $65M-$70M without new rules. (WDRB, Nov. 5 · WBRZ)

Our numbers on it: in 2024-25, the last year before revenue sharing, Louisville reported $155.9M in revenue and $154.8M in expenses, and LSU reported $223.5M and $219.6M. A $30M gap would be about 19% of Louisville's whole budget. (EADA FY2025)

6. The federal bill is on the clock. The House came back Nov. 9 for its lame-duck session with the Protect College Sports Act still waiting; the Senate passed it 77-22 on Sept. 28. AP reports Democrats won at least 247 House seats in the midterms, and most House Democrats oppose the bill. If it isn't law by Jan. 3, it starts over. SEC commissioner Greg Sankey: "Elections have consequences." (AP · On3)
