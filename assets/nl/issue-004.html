The big story: the men's basketball money, school by school

College basketball tipped off this week, so we pulled two years of men's basketball numbers from the federal filings, 2023-24 (FY2024) and 2024-25 (FY2025), for all 68 Power 4 schools plus Notre Dame (68 total). Source: U.S. Dept. of Education EADA, 2024 and 2025 data · Our scoreboard

The headline: men's basketball revenue across the 68 schools rose from $1.15B to $1.26B, up $104M (9.0%). Spending rose from $924.6M to $1.01B, up $88.5M (9.6%). The typical (median) program brought in $16.3M and spent $14.2M.

Two schools are in their own league. Duke ($52.7M) and Indiana ($52.3M) reported more men's basketball revenue than anyone, and it's about 29% of each school's entire athletic department revenue. Nobody else topped $40M.

The top 5 in men's basketball revenue, FY2025:

  • Duke: $44.8M to $52.7M (+$7.9M)
  • Indiana: $30.6M to $52.3M (+$21.7M, +71%)
  • North Carolina: $32.0M to $39.7M (+$7.7M)
  • Tennessee: $26.8M to $35.3M (+$8.5M)
  • Illinois: $36.2M to $34.8M (−$1.4M)

Biggest jump: Indiana, +$21.7M. The Hoosiers' basketball revenue grew more than any other school's, and their spending rose $12.0M to $31.8M, nearly tied with Duke ($31.9M) for the most in the Power 4. Next-biggest revenue gains: BYU (+$9.6M), Tennessee (+$8.5M) and SMU (+$8.4M).

Biggest "profit" on paper: North Carolina. UNC reported $39.7M in revenue against $16.7M in spending, a $23.0M gap, the widest in the Power 4. Duke ($20.9M) and Indiana ($20.5M) were next.

Seven programs reported spending more than they brought in. Texas had the biggest gap (−$4.8M: $17.4M revenue, $22.2M spent), then Oklahoma (−$4.5M) and Oregon (−$2.4M), followed by Virginia Tech, Cal, Boston College and Northwestern, each under $1M.

Kentucky's number fell, but read the fine print. Kentucky's reported revenue dropped $9.6M, from $30.9M to $21.4M, the biggest decline. But Kentucky reported revenue exactly equal to spending both years, so the drop mirrors lower reported spending, not necessarily fewer fans.

⚠️ Read this before you dunk on anyone. Revenue minus expenses is NOT profit. 22 of the 68 programs reported basketball revenue exactly equal to expenses in FY2025, including Kentucky, Arizona, SMU, Florida State and Clemson. At those schools, "revenue" can include money the school moved in to cover the bill. And these are 2024-25 numbers, the last season before schools started paying players directly. Treat this as what each program reported, not who's winning.

NIL & revenue-sharing news

1. Florida State fired Mike Norvell and owes him about $47M to $51M. FSU made the move Sunday, Nov. 1, with the team 3-5. On3 puts the buyout at about $51M, the third-largest in college football history behind Jimbo Fisher ($76.8M) and Brian Kelly; USA TODAY puts it at $47.1M. The contract requires Norvell to look for another job, which could reduce what FSU pays. On3's sources put the total cost of firing him and his staff and making a new hire at around $100M. (Florida State Athletics, Nov. 1 · On3, Nov. 1 · FSU Wire/USA TODAY, Nov. 2)

Our numbers on it: FSU football reported $83.5M in revenue and $83.3M in expenses for 2024-25, essentially break-even. A $51M buyout would be 61% of that whole football budget (57% at $47.1M) and about 26% of FSU's $192.7M department revenue. For comparison, last week's three firings (Baylor, NC State, Maryland) ran about 25% to 29% of each school's football budget. (EADA FY2025)

2. The Big Ten didn't wait for Congress: a $49M cap, effective now. On Oct. 28, Big Ten presidents approved a rule limiting each school to arranging no more than $27.5M in third-party NIL deals. Added to the direct-pay cap from the House settlement, that's about $49M per school, matching the Protect College Sports Act. Violations bring escalating fines, and athletes' own NIL deals that the school doesn't arrange aren't covered. Lawyers for the athletes in the House settlement say the rule violates that agreement. (Big Ten, Oct. 28 · AP, Oct. 28)

Our numbers on it: $49M is 38% of Maryland's $128.7M in 2024-25 athletic revenue, the lowest in the Big Ten, but only 17% of Ohio State's $295.3M. Same cap, very different bite. (EADA FY2025)

3. The election made the federal bill's path harder. Democrats are on track to win control of the House after Tuesday's midterms, On3 reports, citing calls by CNN and NBC News. So when the House returns Nov. 9 for the first time since the Senate passed the Protect College Sports Act 77-22, it'll be a lame-duck session. On3 counts eight scheduled working days through Nov. 20, then Nov. 30 to Dec. 17. If the bill isn't law when the new Congress is sworn in Jan. 3, it starts over. (On3, Nov. 4 · Congress.gov)

4. Iowa set another budget record, and Iowa State needed $12.5M of help. Board of Regents reports made public Tuesday show Iowa athletics spent a record $170.3M in fiscal 2026, $655,503 over budget, and its Learfield multimedia contract brought in $5.9M instead of the budgeted $10M. Iowa State overspent its budget by $4.4M and took $12.5M in university and foundation support to balance. (The Gazette, Nov. 3)

Our numbers on it: in the federal filings for 2024-25, Iowa reported $180.0M in revenue and $150.9M in expenses, while Iowa State reported $103.9M and $103.9M, matched almost to the dollar. Those filings use a different method than state budget reports, so don't compare the two directly. (EADA FY2025)
