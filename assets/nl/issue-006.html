The big story: what schools spend to recruit, school by school

Football's early signing period opens Dec. 2, and the transfer portal follows. So we pulled every Power 4 school's (plus Notre Dame's) reported recruiting expenses for two years, 2023-24 (FY2024) and 2024-25 (FY2025). That's the money for recruits' and coaches' travel, lodging, meals, phone calls and official and unofficial visits. Source: U.S. Dept. of Education EADA, 2024 and 2025 data · What counts as "recruiting expenses" (34 CFR 668.47) · Our scoreboard

The headline: the 68 schools spent $231.0M on recruiting in FY2025, up just $3.9M (1.7%) from $227.1M. Total athletic spending at the same schools rose 9.6%, from $10.61B to $11.62B. Recruiting slipped from 2.1% to 2.0% of the budget. The typical (median) school spent $3.18M, up from $3.11M. 36 schools spent more, and 32 spent less.

The top 5 recruiting budgets, FY2025:

  • USC: $6.78M (up from $5.92M, +$0.86M)

  • Miami: $6.48M (up from $6.40M, +$0.08M)

  • Oklahoma: $6.39M (up from $5.88M, +$0.50M)

  • Alabama: $6.16M (down from $7.10M, −$0.93M)

  • Michigan: $6.06M (up from $4.05M, +$2.01M)

The smallest: Iowa State ($1.46M), Houston ($1.50M), Colorado ($1.87M), Wake Forest ($1.94M) and Kansas State ($1.95M). USC spent about 4.6x what Iowa State did.

The SEC spends the most and cut the most. The typical SEC school spent $4.41M, vs. $3.23M in the Big Ten, $2.91M in the ACC and $2.39M in the Big 12. But SEC recruiting spending fell 1.4% overall ($71.6M to $70.6M), and 9 of 16 SEC schools cut. The Big 12 grew the fastest (+8.0%), then the ACC (+4.9%). The Big Ten was flat.

Biggest increases: Michigan (+$2.01M, +50%), SMU (+$1.49M, +95%), Auburn (+$1.36M, +45%), BYU (+$0.99M, +44%) and North Carolina (+$0.95M, +29%).

Biggest cuts: Texas A&M (−$1.45M, −23%), Georgia (−$1.41M, −22%), Vanderbilt (−$1.01M, −26%), Alabama (−$0.93M, −13%) and Washington (−$0.90M, −21%).

The gender gap is wide. Men's teams got $177.0M (77%) of recruiting money, and women's teams got $54.0M (23%), even though women are 46% of the athletes at these schools (18,562 of 40,059). That works out to about $8,230 per male athlete vs. $2,910 per female athlete. The gap is closing slowly: women's recruiting spending rose 8.4% in a year, while men's was flat (−0.1%).

  • Biggest women's share: Stanford (38%), Pitt (36%), Louisville (34%)

  • Smallest: USC (13%), Alabama (14%), Miami (15%)

⚠️ Read this before you crown anyone. These are 2024-25 numbers, the last year before schools started paying players directly, and EADA "recruiting expenses" don't include NIL deals or revenue-sharing money. Today, those are where most of the real recruiting money goes. Think of this as the travel-and-visits budget, not the price of a recruiting class. And schools report these costs a little differently from one another.

NIL & revenue-sharing news

1. The SEC floated a luxury tax with real numbers. Yahoo Sports' Ross Dellenger reports the SEC is pitching a tiered fine system for roster spending over the $21.6M revenue-share cap. Fines start under 1% below $50.5M, then 1% from $50.5M to $60.5M, 3% from $60.5M to $70.5M, and 10% above $70.5M. That means a school could spend $70.5M with a fine of about $7M. The rates rise each year, reaching 20% for the biggest spenders in year three. The plan would count some school-affiliated third-party NIL deals as "institutional pay," and a retroactive Oct. 1 start date is under discussion. One Power 4 AD summed it up: the cap would "basically" be $70M. (Yahoo Sports/On3, Nov. 11 · Sports Business Journal, Nov. 12 · Athletic Business, Nov. 12)

Our numbers on it: $70.5M is about 34% of the typical SEC school's 2024-25 athletic revenue ($204.9M median). That ranges from 21% of Texas's revenue to 48% of Vanderbilt's. (EADA FY2025)

2. A former Big 12 AD called the NIL system "money laundering." At a Knight Commission event in Washington this week, former Iowa State AD Jamie Pollard said, "Our industry right now is laundering money," describing donations steered to school-affiliated businesses that then sign third-party NIL deals with players. He warned that "we're sitting on the next savings and loans crisis in higher education." (AP · Yahoo Sports/The Comeback, Nov. 17 · Athletic Business, Nov. 18)

Our numbers on it: Pollard's old school reported $103.9M in revenue and $103.9M in expenses for 2024-25, and the smallest recruiting budget in the Power 4 ($1.46M). (EADA FY2025)

3. Boards are asking harder questions. On Nov. 11, Iowa's Board of Regents said it will take a harder look at athletics budgets. Regent Christine Hensley noted UNI is running a deficit, Iowa State is drawing on reserves and Iowa is making minimum loan payments. Iowa's CFO: "revenue share is real." (Iowa Capital Dispatch, Nov. 11) At a trustees' conference near D.C., a UNC System official said the system's 15 campuses spent $640M on sports in fiscal 2025 but brought in just $412M from athletics, and none of its programs were self-sufficient, including UNC Chapel Hill. (Inside Higher Ed, Nov. 13)

Our numbers on it: that's why "revenue minus expenses" in federal filings isn't profit. UNC Chapel Hill reported 2024-25 revenue exactly equal to expenses ($180.8M each), and the filings count school support and student fees as revenue. (EADA FY2025)

4. Washington sold its stadium name for a record $70M. Husky Stadium becomes Columbia Bank Stadium on Jan. 1, 2027, under a 10-year deal (pending regents' approval). At $7M a year, it's the largest football stadium naming-rights deal in college sports. (UW Athletics, Nov. 12 · Sports Business Journal, Nov. 12)

Our numbers on it: $7M a year is about 3.9% of UW's $178.4M in 2024-25 athletic revenue, and more than double the $3.35M it spent on recruiting that year. (EADA FY2025)

5. Texas A&M's buyout math: $56.4M. After a third straight loss dropped the Aggies to 4-6, USA TODAY reports that firing Mike Elko on Dec. 1 would cost $56.4M, the second-largest buyout ever behind the $77M Texas A&M owed Jimbo Fisher. Elko's 2026 base salary: $10.75M. (USA TODAY, Nov. 14)

Our numbers on it: $56.4M is about 94% of what Texas A&M spent on football in 2024-25 ($60.2M), and about 11.5x its whole recruiting budget ($4.90M, down 23% from the year before). (EADA FY2025)

6. Stanford launched a new NIL shop. Stanford announced a sponsorship agreement with Redwood Brand Partners, a new independent NIL organization led by former UVA NIL general manager Clay Walker, which began operating Nov. 17. Stanford says it is now "the only pathway for direct student-athlete NIL support," since the Lifetime Cardinal collective is no longer operating. (Stanford Athletics, Nov. 17)

Our numbers on it: Stanford reported $192.8M in athletic revenue in 2024-25, and it puts the biggest share of its recruiting money into women's teams of any Power 4 school (38% of $2.75M). (EADA FY2025)

7. The federal bill's last push. Supporters of the Protect College Sports Act are lobbying, writing letters and running ads to get a House vote in the lame-duck session. If it doesn't reach the president before the new Congress is sworn in in January, it starts over. Florida Rep. Byron Donalds: "I hate that bill." (Front Office Sports, Nov. 12 · Inside Higher Ed, Nov. 13)

Catching up: the 24-team Playoff is official. On Nov. 9, the CFP approved a 24-team field starting in 2027-28, with ESPN as the exclusive broadcaster. ESPN reports the deal is worth nearly $1.7B over five years, about $345M a year on top of the ~$1.3B it already pays, but ESPN gets about $140M a year back because the SEC, Big 12 and ACC title games go away. (CFP, Nov. 9 · ESPN)

Our numbers on it: our 68 schools reported $5.55B in football revenue in 2024-25. The new $345M a year is about 6% of that. (EADA FY2025)

Quick hit: the SEC fined Arkansas $500,000 after fans stormed the field following its Nov. 14 win over South Carolina. That's about 0.26% of Arkansas's $195.9M in 2024-25 revenue. (SEC, Nov. 15 · AP · EADA FY2025)
